## What this campaign decides

**No default.** AntHocNet does not run in it. It measures three protocols
that AntHocNet's own lineage is usually compared with. They live in the
`adaptive` module
([`ns3/baselines/adaptive/`](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/tree/main/ns3/baselines/adaptive)):

| arm | issue | source | kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ara` | [#586](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/issues/586) | Günes, Sorges & Bouazizi, ICDCS-W 2002 | ant-colony, on demand |
| `termite` | [#587](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/issues/587) | Roth, PhD dissertation, Cornell 2005, ch. 4 | stigmergic, pheromone on every packet |
| `qrouting` | [#588](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/issues/588) | Boyan & Littman, NIPS 1994 | learned (Q-learning on delivery time) |

A comparator's numbers count only after it clears three gates (AGENTS.md,
Conventions):

1. a fidelity sheet from its paper (the module
   [README](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/blob/main/ns3/baselines/adaptive/README.md));
2. the per-PR delivery smoke ([#439](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/issues/439));
3. an anchor that reproduces its own paper's trend.

This campaign runs gate 3, then a family comparison for the arms that pass.
An arm whose anchor fails ships with a written infeasibility verdict on its
issue instead of family numbers.

## Part 1: anchors

Ten seeds per cell, on `anthocnet-compare` at ns-3.42. Each rule is fixed
here. A result that misses its rule is reported as a miss, with no re-run
under changed settings.

### ARA: pause sweep against AODV

The paper's setup (§4): 50 nodes, 1500 × 300 m, random waypoint at up to
10 m/s, 900 s, 10 CBR flows. Pauses are 0, 30, 120 and 600 s, out of the
paper's seven. Its findings: under high mobility, ARA delivers more than
95 % while AODV does not. Below roughly 100 s of pause, ARA has the least
overhead. From 300 to 600 s, AODV and ARA are close.

**Rule.** Paired by seed, ARA minus AODV:

- **PDR**: positive with the 95 % CI excluding zero at pause 0 and at
  pause 30;
- **NRL**: negative with the CI excluding zero at pause 0.

The 120 and 600 s cells are reported, not gated.

### Termite: goodput and path length against speed

The trend comes from §4.4 of Roth's dissertation, which evaluates the
chapter-4 Termite this arm implements. §7.3 evaluates ReTermite, a different
protocol, so it is not this arm's anchor.

§4.4's setup is 100 nodes and 600 s, with zero pause after a 60 s start.
Every node sends a 64-byte packet every 0.5 s and receives an
acknowledgement for each. The field is 50 × 50 m at 10 m range, with a
perfect MAC. ns-3 has no perfect MAC. The anchor therefore keeps the field
*in ranges*: 1250 × 1250 m at 250 m, the same 5 × 5 ranges.

Traffic is ten pairs of 64-byte flows at 1024 bit/s, each with a reply flow
standing in for the acknowledgement
([fixture](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/blob/main/ns3/tools/fixtures/termite/termite-anchor.flows)).
Speeds are 1, 5, 10 and 20 m/s.

§4.4.2 finds that data goodput declines as mobility rises, and that
delivered data packets take sharply longer paths while route request and
reply paths stay short. §4.4 has no reference protocol; AODV runs alongside
for context only.

**Rule.** Comparing 20 m/s against 1 m/s, as a difference of means with a
Welch 95 % CI:

- Termite's PDR falls, with the CI excluding zero;
- its mean hop count over delivered data (`##ROUTE##`, last field) rises,
  with the CI excluding zero.

The 5 and 10 m/s cells are reported, not gated.

### Q-routing: low against high load, against the oracle

Boyan & Littman's finding (§3) is that at low load Q-routing settles on the
shortest paths. At high load it routes around congestion and delivers faster
than shortest-path routing.

The anchor is a static field: 36 nodes in 600 × 600 m, 175 m range,
pause > run time, 300 s, 10 flows. It runs at two loads, 2048 and
65536 bit/s per flow, against the shortest-hop oracle.

**Rule.** Paired by seed, Q-routing minus oracle:

- at **low load**, the PDR CI lies wholly above −1 pp;
- at **high load**, mean delay is negative with the CI excluding zero.

## Part 2: family comparison

The family comparison follows ADR-0026: each arm runs where it is native.

- **`ara` and `termite`** are MANET protocols. They run the six MANET grid
  cells.
- **`qrouting`** is not tied to a family. It also runs the static mesh,
  FANET (main and sparse), VANET (main and sparse) and disaster cells.

The arms run alone, on the v2.4.0 control's command lines and seeds
(20 per cell: 10 for each grid block, 5 × 4 for the static mesh). Their rows
are then paired by seed with the committed v2.4.0 rows,
`results/cells/<cell>-v240.txt`. That works because the v2.5.0 scale screen's
recheck reproduced those rows byte for byte (see
[sweeps/scale.md](sweeps/scale.md)). So a v2.5.0 arm row and a v2.4.0 row with
the same seed saw the same mobility and traffic.

Each arm cell is read three ways, as arm minus reference, with
`bench_parse.paired_stats`:

1. against **AntHocNet**;
2. against the cell's **oracle**;
3. against the cell's **best native** baseline: the one
   [`native_view.py`](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/blob/main/tools/bench/native_view.py)
   picks (highest mean PDR, ties broken on delay99).

These readings are descriptive, with no pass/fail rule. They answer one
question: where does each lineage stand against AntHocNet and the natives,
and in which families?

**Out of scope.** The satellite (ISL grid, LEO) and SAGIN cells run on the
`isl-grid` and `leo-walker` harnesses. Those harnesses do not carry the
`adaptive` module, so the arms have no satellite numbers in v2.5.0.

### Anchors

**ARA vs AODV** (10 seeds, paired):

| pause | ARA PDR | AODV PDR | dPDR | d NRL | d delay99 | rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 98.42 % | 89.27 % | **+9.15 [+8.44, +9.86]** | **−49.5 [−50.8, −48.3]** | +611 ms | PDR ✔ NRL ✔ |
| 30 | 98.54 % | 89.55 % | **+8.99 [+7.06, +10.92]** | −47.2 [−48.5, −45.9] | +652 ms | PDR ✔ |
| 120 | 98.50 % | 90.24 % | +8.26 [+7.07, +9.45] | −40.5 [−42.5, −38.4] | +709 ms | reported |
| 600 | 99.54 % | 94.52 % | +5.02 [+3.91, +6.13] | −31.1 [−34.4, −27.8] | −196 ms | reported |

The paper's trend reproduces:

- ARA stays above 95 % delivery at every pause, and AODV does not reach it.
- ARA has the least overhead under high mobility.

What the paper does not show is ARA's tail delay: 0.6 to 0.7 s higher at
short pauses. Packets wait while the BANT retraces the FANT's path, and again
when they are re-routed after a MAC or ARP drop.

**Q-routing vs the shortest-hop oracle** (10 seeds, paired):

| load | Q-routing PDR | oracle PDR | dPDR | d mean delay | rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2048 bit/s/flow | 99.99 % | 99.65 % | +0.34 [−0.45, +1.13] | — | PDR CI above −1 pp ✔ |
| 65536 bit/s/flow | 27.20 % | 49.74 % | −22.54 [−25.93, −19.15] | **+158 ms [+65, +251]** | mean delay below 0 ✘ |

The low-load half of Boyan & Littman's trend holds: Q-routing settles on
shortest paths. The high-load half does not. Their gain comes from routing
around *queues* in a wired network. Here the shared channel is past
saturation: the oracle itself loses 49.7 % of packets on the channel and
Q-routing 73.4 %, so a detour adds transmissions to the contended medium
instead of relieving a queue.

Per the rule, the arm ships with this verdict and its family numbers do not
count. The six Q-routing-only family cells (static mesh, FANET ×2, VANET ×2,
disaster) were withdrawn before dispatch. The learned comparator that is
designed for a wireless channel is [#756](https://github.com/danieljoppi/AntHocNet/issues/756).

**Termite: speed trend** (Roth §4.4; 10 seeds per speed, two-way flows):

| speed | Termite PDR | Termite data hops | AODV PDR (context) | AODV hops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 m/s | 47.45 % | 6.65 | 78.25 % | 3.11 |
| 5 m/s | 45.43 % | 6.96 | 72.79 % | 3.17 |
| 10 m/s | 43.75 % | 7.16 | 70.91 % | 3.11 |
| 20 m/s | 42.54 % | 7.94 | 67.05 % | 3.13 |

Rule: 20 m/s minus 1 m/s, Welch 95 % CI.

| metric | difference | rule | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| PDR | −4.91 pp [−10.36, +0.54] | must fall, CI excluding 0 | ✘ |
| data hops | +1.29 [−0.38, +2.96] | must rise, CI excluding 0 | ✘ |

Both quantities move the way §4.4 reports, monotonically at every speed step.
Neither difference separates from zero at ten seeds, so the anchor is a
**miss**. As pre-registered, there is no re-run with more seeds.

The arm ships with this verdict, and its family numbers below do not count.
Two things limit the reproduction:

- ns-3 has no perfect MAC, which §4.4's goodput-against-speed curve relies on.
- The per-packet acknowledgement is modelled as a reply flow.

Seed-to-seed spread is large: Termite's PDR SD is 7.2 pp at 1 m/s and 3.6 pp at 20 m/s.

**What it says:**

- **ARA is the strongest comparator AntHocNet has met on the grid.**
  - On the two-ray cells it delivers 0.9 to 1.4 pp more, at a fifth of
    AntHocNet's routing overhead (NRL about 1.6 against about 7).
  - It pays for that with a 99th-percentile delay near 0.9 s higher, the cost
    of holding packets for its route discovery.
  - On a fading (Nakagami) channel ARA loses: 2.2 to 6.8 pp less delivery and
    tail delays of 9 s. AntHocNet's proactive maintenance and multipath keep
    working when links flicker, where ARA's purely reactive repair does not.
  - ARA beats the best native protocol in every cell (+1.8 to +12.7 pp).
- **Termite** (not counted: its anchor missed) trails every protocol on the
  grid under one-way CBR. Its data
  walks until a seed or a reply lays the destination's trail. On the two-ray
  cells 29 to 31 % of data packets die on TTL; on the Nakagami cells about
  30 % die at the MAC and 30 % on the channel (the per-packet book, #761). This is what chapter 4 of Roth's dissertation
  implies for silent sinks; it is not an implementation defect (see the
  anchor above for the two-way regime).
